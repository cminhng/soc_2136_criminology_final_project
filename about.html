about this project

Written, coded, and illustrated by Christine Nguyen.

This project is an interactive, choice-based website that simulates how life experiences across four developmental stages—childhood (ages 0-10), early adolescence (ages 11-14), late adolescence (15-18), and early adulthood (19-25)—can influence criminal behavior.

(The reason the stages are laid out as such is because crime peaks in late adolescence to early adulthood, so mid- to later-adulthood is not explored.)

The simulation is grounded primarily in Hirschi’s Social Bond Theory (1969), with Agnew’s General Strain Theory (1992) as a complementary framework.

Thus, each choice strengthens or weakens key social bonds and alters levels of strain. The final outcome reflects how cumulative social bonds and strain shape conformity or deviance.

To make these outcomes deterministic for the purpose of this game, the player’s scores are measured in five different dimensions: attachment, commitment, involvement, belief, and strain. These scores have neutral default values at the beginning of playthrough, and by the end, each choice the player makes will have modified these scores. The resulting tally dictates what ending the player receives.

SPOILERS (narrative decisions I made and why)

  • Limited choices in CHILDHOOD stage: Some components of the social bond, namely attachment to parental figures, are outside of our control. I had the player start with a disadvantaged state (-2 attachment and +1 strain) to reflect this. However, later choices and other sources of bond-building can offset these initial circumstances.
  • Allowed player to choose desistance or persistance after deliquent behavior in LATE ADOLESCENT stage: I wanted the player to have stronger agency and to reflect the idea that people can change and are not locked onto their path (introduced by the age-graded theory of informal social control by Sampson and Laub).
  • I use both Agnew's GST and Hirschi's Social Bond Theories so that I could add an extra dimension (strain) to my calculations. The two theories do have conflicting beliefs, so while Hirschi's theory suited my concept more, I didn't fully buy into the idea that just because an individual doesn't have a strong attachment to society they will commit crime. Merton's Adaptations to Strain theory provides more compelling alternatives. For example, retreatists are not necessarily criminals despite rejecting conventional goals and not having access to conventional means.
